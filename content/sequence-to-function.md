You hand the model a stretch of DNA and ask a blunt question: what does this sequence *do*? Not what protein it might encode, but how the living cell reads it, which genes it turns on and how strongly, which stretches it holds open, which regulatory proteins dock on it, how it folds in three dimensions, and, crucially, in *which cell type* all of that happens. This is the regulatory-lobe counterpart to structure prediction (Chapter 8): there the target was a fold, here it is a stack of functional readouts along the genome. The problem is called **sequence-to-function**, and it is genuinely hard for two reasons the rest of the book has been setting up. Regulation acts over long distances, so the sequence that controls a gene can sit a hundred kilobases away. And regulation is cell-type-specific, so the same DNA does different things in a neuron and a T cell. The models that attempt this have gotten strikingly good at some versions of the question and remain honestly stuck on others, and telling those apart is the whole point of the chapter.

## The problem: a window of DNA, a stack of tracks

Only about 2% of the human genome codes for protein (Chapter 4); the rest includes the regulatory machinery that decides when and where each gene fires. A **cis-regulatory** element is a stretch of DNA that controls a nearby gene's expression rather than being transcribed into protein itself. The two workhorses are the **promoter**, the region right at a gene's start where transcription is switched on, and the **enhancer**, a short element that boosts a gene's expression and can act from far away, looping through 3D space to reach a promoter tens or hundreds of kilobases distant. A single **transcription factor (TF)** is a protein that binds specific short DNA motifs to activate or repress transcription, and which TFs are present is part of what makes a cell type a cell type.

The output the model must produce is not one number. It is the same menu of coverage tracks you met in the data primer (Chapter 4): RNA-seq for expression, ATAC or DNase for accessibility, ChIP-seq for TF and histone binding, CAGE for transcription start activity, Hi-C for 3D contacts, each a signal value along the genome. And there is a second axis: **cell-type-specific** means one such track *per cell type or tissue*, because open chromatin in liver differs from open chromatin in brain. So the target is a two-dimensional stack, assay by cell type, and the model reads a window of raw DNA and predicts the whole stack at once. The cell type is not an input the model is told; it is an output dimension it predicts in parallel, one track per context it was trained on.

!!! intuition "Intuition"
    Think of the genome as sheet music and each cell type as a different instrument: the sequence-to-function model reads the score once and predicts how loudly every instrument plays every bar.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *"If I only care about one gene's expression in one tissue, why does the model predict thousands of tracks?"* Because the shared structure is what makes it learn. A model forced to predict accessibility, binding, and expression across many cell types at once must discover the regulatory grammar, which motifs open chromatin, which combinations drive a promoter, rather than memorizing one readout. The many-task target is a feature, not overhead, and it is why these models transfer to variants they never saw. That transfer is exactly what Chapter 13 leans on to score mutations.

## Models that attempt it

Two model families both claim the phrase "genomic foundation model," and conflating them is the most common mistake a newcomer makes. They differ in the one thing that matters most: what they are trained on.

The first family is the **supervised sequence-to-function** line, and it is the one that actually predicts tracks. **Enformer** was the breakthrough: a transformer that takes a roughly 200-kilobase input window, with an effective reach of about 100 kilobases, and predicts thousands of tracks in 128-base-pair bins, finally capturing enhancers acting from far away [@avsec2021]. **Borzoi**, from Calico's Kelley lab, pushes the target to RNA-seq coverage at 32-base-pair resolution, four times sharper than Enformer, and from that finer signal it scores transcription, splicing, and polyadenylation in one model [@linder2025]. **AlphaGenome**, DeepMind's successor to Enformer, takes a full one-megabase window and predicts many track types at **up to single-base resolution** (base-pair for RNA-seq and accessibility, coarser for ChIP-seq and contact maps), and it matches or beats the best prior models on 25 of 26 variant-effect evaluations [@avsec2026]. Two more fill out the family: **Sei** predicts tens of thousands of chromatin profiles and distills them into interpretable "sequence classes" of regulatory activity [@chen2022], and **Decima**, from Genentech and built on the Borzoi lineage, predicts expression at cell-type resolution by training on tens of millions of single cells aggregated into pseudobulk profiles [@lal2026].

Here is the precision point. Every model just named is a **supervised multi-output regressor**: DNA goes in, and measured experimental tracks are the *labels* it is trained to reproduce. It is not self-supervised, and it did not learn from unlabeled sequence the way a language model does. Calling it a "foundation model" is loose usage, however common. The genuine self-supervised foundation models of the genome are the second family, the **DNA language models**, trained to predict masked or next nucleotides from raw sequence with no experimental labels at all (the recipe from Chapters 1 and 3). **DNABERT-2** [@zhou2023] and the **Nucleotide Transformer** [@dallatorre2025] are BERT-style masked models over multi-species DNA; **HyenaDNA** [@nguyen2023] and **Caduceus** [@schiff2024] swap attention for long-convolution and state-space architectures to reach up to a megabase at single-nucleotide resolution; and **Evo** [@nguyen2024] and its successor **Evo 2**, trained on over nine trillion nucleotides across all domains of life, both predict and generate genomic sequence [@brixi2026]. These produce general-purpose representations, but on the specific task of predicting regulatory tracks, the supervised line still generally wins. The two families are converging, and which one to reach for depends on whether you want a track prediction or a reusable embedding.

The supervised family has been climbing two axes at once. Older intuition said context and resolution trade off, that seeing farther means seeing coarser. The last few years broke that: each generation reached both farther along the genome and finer in its output, from Enformer's 128-base-pair bins over a roughly 200-kilobase window to AlphaGenome's single bases over a megabase. Longer context lets the model see distal enhancers; finer resolution lets it place a splice site or a start codon exactly.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Do not call Enformer, Borzoi, or AlphaGenome "self-supervised" or lump them with the DNA language models. They are supervised regressors fit to experimental tracks; their skill comes from labels, not from pretraining on unlabeled DNA. The confusion matters because it predicts the wrong failure modes: a supervised track model is bounded by the assays and cell types in its training set, not by a pretraining corpus.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *"Which one should I actually run?"* If you want predicted regulatory tracks or a variant-effect score, reach for the supervised line, AlphaGenome or Borzoi. If you want reusable sequence embeddings, work in a non-human species with few labeled tracks, or need to *generate* sequence, a DNA language model like Evo 2 or Nucleotide Transformer is the tool. They answer different questions, and a leaderboard for one says little about the other (Chapter 16).

## What they do well and what is still hard

Be precise about the win, because it is real. These models predict variation **across genes and across tissues** impressively well. Give AlphaGenome a promoter it has never seen and it will rank its expression sensibly; ask which sequences drive open chromatin in the liver and it recovers the motifs; score a large panel of regulatory variants and the best models now top the benchmarks. For the question "how does this class of sequence behave relative to that class," the field passes.

Now the honest gap, and it is the one a population geneticist will press you on. The same models explain **inter-individual variation**, the differences between two specific people's genomes, poorly. Benchmarked on personal transcriptomes, where the task is to predict how one person's expression of a gene differs from another's given their actual genotypes, current models perform badly and sometimes get even the *direction* of a cis-regulatory effect wrong [@huang2023; @sasse2023]. The reason is structural, not a bug to be patched. A model trained to explain expression differences *across the genome* learns from the large sequence differences between distinct loci; the differences between two individuals at the *same* locus are a few scattered variants, a signal orders of magnitude smaller and outside what the training objective rewarded. Cross-gene skill and personal-genome skill are different tests, and topping the first does not deliver the second. This sits directly under any population-cohort application, and it is an open frontier, not a solved problem, that Chapter 13 takes up when it turns these models into variant scorers.

Several related limits cluster nearby. **Enhancer-to-gene linking** stays hard: the model may predict an enhancer is active without pinning down which gene it controls, since the target can be the fifth promoter over, not the nearest. **Cell-type coverage** is bounded by the assays in the training set, so a cell type or condition that was never profiled is off the map. And the deepest gap is conceptual: a predicted track is a *molecular* readout, not a phenotype. Knowing that a variant shifts a coverage track by some amount is a long way from knowing it causes disease, because most traits are massively polygenic and a single regulatory change is one small push among thousands [@boyle2017]. Predicting the track is a genuine milestone; predicting the phenotype is a different and larger problem, and closing that distance is part of what multimodal integration (Chapter 14) is reaching for.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *"Your model nails GTEx expression. Can I use it to interpret my patient's noncoding variant?"* Cautiously, and not on its own. The model's strength is cross-gene and cross-tissue prediction; personal, single-locus effects are exactly where it is weakest and can invert the sign. Use it to nominate and prioritize, then confirm with orthogonal evidence, an eQTL colocalization or an MPRA (Chapter 5), before you build a clinical story on it.

!!! intuition "Intuition"
    A sequence-to-function model is a superb reader of the genome's regulatory grammar and a shaky predictor of what any one person's edits to that grammar will do; hold both facts at once and you will use it well.
