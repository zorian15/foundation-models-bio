Appendix A handed you a cell's full transcriptome, but at a price that is easy to miss: to run single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq, Appendix A) you first dissolve the tissue into a suspension of loose cells. You learn what each cell was expressing and lose where it was sitting. A neuron and the blood vessel it wraps, a killer T cell and the tumor cell it is touching, the layered architecture of a cortex — all of it becomes a bag of anonymized profiles. Spatial omics buys the coordinates back. It measures expression without cutting the tissue apart, so the map survives alongside the readout. This appendix is a short tour: what that recovered location buys you, and why the models that use it are a younger, shakier field than the protein and genome models in the main book.

## What spatial data adds

Start with what dissociation throws away. **Spatial transcriptomics** is the family of assays that measure gene expression while recording the physical position in the tissue that each measurement came from. The output is the same cell-by-gene (or spot-by-gene) matrix scRNA-seq gives you, plus one thing: an (x, y) coordinate for every row. That coordinate is the whole point, because a cell's biology is not just its own contents but its address.

Location buys three things that a dissociated profile cannot recover. The first is the **neighborhood**: which cells physically touch which. The second is **cell-cell communication (ligand-receptor analysis)**, inferring signaling by checking whether a cell expressing a signaling molecule sits next to one expressing its receptor — a guess from dissociated data, a direct observation when you know who is adjacent. The third is **tissue architecture**: the recurring regional structure of a tissue, like the layers of a cortex or the core, margin, and stroma of a tumor. A cell's *type* is intrinsic and clustering recovers it from dissociated data; a cell's *niche* is relational, and two cells of the identical type behave differently depending on what surrounds them. Spatial data is also a natural partner for the tissue's histology image of the same section, which makes it one more modality to fuse (multimodal integration, Chapter 14).

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *My scRNA-seq clustering already gives me cell types, and ligand-receptor tools already infer which cells talk. What does measured location actually add?* Inferred communication tells you which cell types *could* signal if they were adjacent; spatial data tells you which cells *are* adjacent, turning an inverse problem into an observation. Where it leaks: spatial assays pay for that with their own limits — smaller gene panels or lower sensitivity — so the coordinate is not free, it is bought against coverage.

Spatial data arrives in two flavors, and the split governs everything downstream. **Imaging-based** methods (MERFISH [@chen2015], and the commercial Xenium and CosMx, all descended from single-molecule fluorescence imaging) light up individual transcripts in place, giving subcellular resolution and true single cells — but only for a *targeted panel* of a few hundred to a few thousand genes chosen before the experiment. **Sequencing-based** methods (the original spatial-transcriptomics assay behind Visium [@stahl2016], plus Slide-seq [@rodriques2019] and Stereo-seq) capture RNA onto barcoded spots or beads and sequence it, giving the *whole transcriptome* with nothing to choose in advance — but at a spot that has classically pooled several cells. The frontier is eroding this tradeoff from both sides — Visium HD and Stereo-seq now reach near-subcellular spots, and imaging panels have grown past 5,000 genes — yet the classic split still describes what you are most likely to actually have on your desk.

## The models

The platform hands the model opposite problems. On imaging data you have real single cells but only the panel's genes, so a common task is *imputing* the unmeasured transcriptome by borrowing from a matched scRNA-seq reference. On sequencing data you have every gene but each spot is a mixture of cells, so the task is **spatial deconvolution**: estimating which cell types, in what proportions, make up each spot, again anchored to a single-cell reference. Tools like cell2location (deconvolution) and Tangram (single-cell-to-spatial mapping) do this kind of job, and they are what most Visium analyses actually run before any spatial reasoning begins.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Reading a spot as a cell. A 55-micrometer Visium spot pools several cells, so its expression profile is a blend; treating it as one cell's profile — or calling its dominant cluster its "cell type" — invents a cell that was never there. This is exactly why deconvolution exists, and why single-cell claims drawn from spot-based data need care.

The models that earn spatial data its keep are the ones that *use* the location rather than just repair the missing axis. The workhorse primitive is the **neighborhood graph**: connect each cell or spot to its physical neighbors, then let a graph neural network blend a cell's own expression with its neighbors' before it decides anything. That is how **spatial domain** identification works — grouping a tissue into coherent regions, where a spatial domain (or **tissue niche**) is a recurring local arrangement of cell types, like a cortical layer or a tumor margin. SpaGCN [@hu2021], STAGATE, and GraphST are representative, and they differ mostly in how they build and weight that graph. Almost every method in this area is, at bottom, a different recipe for mixing a cell with its surroundings.

!!! intuition "Intuition"
    A spatial model is a single-cell model plus a neighbor graph; nearly every method is a different way of letting a cell's representation absorb who is around it.

The foundation-model wave that reshaped proteins (Chapters 7 and 8) and genomes (Chapter 12) is younger and less settled here. The most prominent attempt is **Nicheformer** [@tejada2025], a transformer pretrained on roughly 110 million cells — dissociated and spatial together — to produce representations that carry spatial context, then fine-tuned for tasks like predicting a cell's neighborhood composition. Others, such as scGPT-spatial and CellPLM, extend an existing single-cell foundation model by adding a position embedding. It is worth being honest about why this lags the protein and genome side. Many "spatial foundation models" are single-cell models with coordinates bolted on, not architectures built around geometry. The data is fragmented: imaging panels differ from dataset to dataset, so there is no clean shared vocabulary to pretrain on the way UniRef gives proteins or a reference genome gives DNA. Benchmarks are still forming, with no single spatial task yet playing the load-bearing, widely trusted role that structure prediction or ProteinGym plays elsewhere. And spatial inherits multimodal integration's central bottleneck (Chapter 14): fully paired data — expression, location, and histology on the very same cells — is the scarcest kind there is.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *Should I reach for a spatial foundation model the way I'd reach for ESM-2 or Evo?* Not yet, and it pays to be precise about it. For most spatial analysis today the graph-based, task-specific methods — domain calling, deconvolution, neighborhood statistics — are what people actually run and trust, and they are strong baselines. The pretrained spatial foundation models are genuinely promising and improving fast, but they are not yet the default first thing you reach for that their protein and genome cousins have become. Treat them as an active frontier (Chapter 18), not a settled instrument.

The mental model to carry out of this appendix: spatial omics restores the one thing dissociation discards, the tissue map, and the modeling around it is a young field organized so far around a single new primitive, the neighbor graph, with foundation models still finding their footing. Cell imaging (Appendix C) offers yet another spatial view of the same cells — their morphology rather than their transcripts — and fusing all of these back into one picture of a tissue is the unfinished work of Chapter 14.
