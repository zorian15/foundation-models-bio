A genetic association is an address, not an explanation. A genome-wide association study (GWAS, Chapter 5) hands you a stretch of the genome where genotype correlates with a disease, but the working scientist needs the next sentence: which variant in that stretch does the work, what does it do inside a cell, and would changing it actually change the disease. That is the variant-to-mechanism problem. It is the hinge between the statistical genetics of Chapter 5 and the molecular models of the rest of the book, the place where a foundation model's variant score finally has to be reconciled with what a population of half a million people actually shows. The shape of a good answer depends almost entirely on where the variant sits and how common it is, so this chapter is organized around that split.

Keep one running example in view: an age-related disease, such as late-onset Alzheimer's, type 2 diabetes, or coronary artery disease, studied in a population cohort of hundreds of thousands of volunteers whose genomes and health records are linked, such as UK Biobank, All of Us, or FinnGen. These cohorts are the raw material of modern human genetics, and aging traits are their sweet spot: common, polygenic, and driven overwhelmingly by variants of individually tiny effect. That last fact is exactly what makes mechanism so hard to pin down here.

## Two variants, two problems

Recall the allele-frequency spectrum (Chapter 5): frequency and effect size are anticorrelated, so the genome offers two very different kinds of disease variant, and they demand two different questions.

The first kind is a rare, large-effect coding variant. The commonest is a **missense variant**, a single-base change that swaps one amino acid for another in a protein. The question it poses is: does this substitution break the protein? That is the property-prediction question of Chapter 7 aimed squarely at human health. Purifying selection keeps genuinely protein-breaking variants rare, so these live in the low-frequency tail, and when they matter, they often matter a lot.

The second kind is a common, small-effect **noncoding variant**: a change outside any protein-coding exon, typically in a regulatory element such as an enhancer or promoter, that tunes how much of a nearby gene gets made rather than altering the protein itself. The great majority of GWAS associations, on the order of nine in ten, fall in this noncoding space [@boyle2017], and each one nudges a trait by a sliver. Here the question is not "does the protein still fold?" but "does this base change how much of some gene is transcribed, in which cell type, and when?"

One complication sits on top of both. A variant that clearly damages a protein does not automatically cause disease in everyone who carries it. **Penetrance** is the fraction of carriers who actually develop the associated phenotype, and for most disease variants it is well below one. Incomplete penetrance is why "this variant is damaging" and "this variant made this person sick" are different claims, a gap that every model in this chapter runs into.

!!! intuition "Intuition"
    A coding variant hands you a *what* — a broken protein — and asks you to find the disease. A noncoding variant hands you a *where* — a nudged dial somewhere in a regulatory network — and asks you to find even the gene.

## Models that attempt it

For coding variants the field is in good shape, because a missense change is a molecular event a model can reason about directly. A family of **missense effect predictors** scores every possible amino-acid substitution for how damaging it is. **AlphaMissense**, an AlphaFold derivative fine-tuned on human and primate population frequencies, scores all roughly 71 million possible human missense variants and calls about 90% of them likely benign or likely pathogenic with useful calibration [@cheng2023]. It sits alongside several methods that share one intuition, that evolution has already run the experiment. **PrimateAI-3D** trains a three-dimensional convolutional network on millions of common variants from 233 primate species, treating "common in primates" as a label for "tolerated in humans" [@gao2023]. **EVE** fits an unsupervised deep generative model to each protein's evolutionary alignment and reads pathogenicity off how surprising a variant is, using no clinical labels at all [@frazer2021]. **CADD** integrates more than 60 genomic annotations into a single deleteriousness score that spans the whole genome, coding and noncoding [@rentzsch2019]. And the zero-shot protein-language-model scoring of Chapter 7, the ESM-1v log-likelihood-ratio recipe [@meier2021], applies unchanged to human missense variants. The machinery is the same one property prediction uses; the target is now a clinical judgment.

Noncoding variants have no residue to swap, so the coding toolkit does not apply, and you reach instead for the sequence-to-function models of Chapter 12. The move is **in-silico mutagenesis**: feed the reference DNA sequence and the mutated sequence through a model like Enformer, Borzoi, or AlphaGenome, and take the difference between the two predicted track sets — expression, chromatin accessibility, splicing — as the variant's predicted regulatory effect [@avsec2021; @linder2025]. AlphaGenome was built specifically to sharpen this regulatory variant-effect prediction across many assays at once [@avsec2026]. The output is a hypothesis: this base, if changed, shifts this gene's expression in this tissue.

Neither kind of score, on its own, tells you a variant is *causal*, because linkage disequilibrium (Chapter 5) means the true driver drags a whole block of correlated neighbors up with it, and any of them can score as damaging. Closing that gap is the job of three statistical tools, and this is where a foundation model's per-variant score becomes an input rather than an answer. **Fine-mapping** uses the LD structure to narrow a region to a small credible set of variants most likely to be causal, and a model's damage score can enter as a functional prior that sharpens the set. **Colocalization** asks whether the GWAS signal and a nearby eQTL — a variant that changes a gene's expression — are driven by the *same* causal variant; when they are, you have named a candidate gene and a direction [@giambartolomei2014]. **Mendelian randomization** goes one step further, using the variant as a randomized instrument to test whether the gene or exposure it affects actually *causes* the disease rather than merely traveling alongside it. Together these turn a ranked list of scored variants into a mechanistic claim: *this* variant changes *this* gene's expression in *this* tissue, and that change moves the disease.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *A statistician asks: your model scores this SNP as highly damaging — doesn't that make it the causal variant?* No. A damage score is a statement about one variant in isolation, but the variant sits in an LD block, so its damaged-looking neighbors score high too, and the real driver need not be the top-scoring one. The score is a prior you feed into fine-mapping and colocalization; causation is what survives after you have accounted for the correlated neighborhood and tied the signal to a gene.

## What they do well and what is still hard

Missense is the genuine success story. AlphaMissense-class predictors cover the entire missense proteome, they are calibrated well enough to help reclassify **variants of uncertain significance** (VUS, the clinical limbo of a variant seen in a patient but not confidently benign or pathogenic), and clinical guidelines now weigh them as one line of evidence. The honest caveat is that "one line of evidence" is exactly right: a strong pathogenicity score is not a diagnosis, and these tools inform a geneticist rather than replace one.

Noncoding regulatory variants are the open frontier, and the gap is large. In-silico mutagenesis with sequence-to-function models predicts the direction and rough size of a regulatory effect far less reliably than a missense predictor calls a coding one; effect sizes are tiny, ground truth is scarce, and the models often disagree. The deepest failure is one this book has flagged before: sequence-to-function models predict differences *across genes* well but explain *inter-individual* variation — how expression differs between two people — poorly, sometimes getting even the sign of a real cis-regulatory effect wrong [@huang2023; @sasse2023]. That is precisely the quantity a population-cohort study of an aging disease depends on, so the models are weakest exactly where the mechanism question is sharpest. Bridging this is a central hope of multimodal integration (Chapter 14).

The other hard problem is not molecular but demographic. Population cohorts are overwhelmingly of European ancestry, and **population stratification** — confounding by ancestry, where allele frequencies and disease rates both differ across groups (Chapter 5) — biases both the associations you discover and the scores you build from them. It surfaces most visibly in a **polygenic score (PRS)**, the sum of a person's trait-associated alleles weighted by their GWAS effect sizes: a PRS trained in one ancestry loses much of its accuracy when applied to another, because the LD patterns and allele frequencies it learned do not transfer [@martin2019]. Deploy such a score clinically and you risk widening the very health disparities it was meant to reduce. The lesson resurfaces in evaluation (Chapter 16): a model that looks accurate can be silently ancestry-specific, and a benchmark that ignores ancestry, or that leaks related individuals between train and test, will not catch it.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Reading a variant-effect score as if it were a causal, portable, population-level statement. It is none of the three: it scores molecular damage, not disease causation; it is computed on one variant while LD hides the real driver nearby; and if the score or the cohort behind it is ancestry-skewed, it may not hold for the patient in front of you. Damage, causation, and portability are three separate bars, and clearing one is not clearing the others.

    *A wet-lab partner asks: we fine-mapped an aging-GWAS locus to one noncoding credible-set variant, and AlphaGenome says it lowers a gene's expression — can we build the program around that?* Treat it as your best hypothesis, not a settled mechanism.
