The recipe behind foundation models carries a set of quiet assumptions about its data: that there is a great deal of it, that it is roughly clean, that a label means what it says, and that the examples you train on are drawn from the same distribution as the ones you will deploy on. Biological data violates all four, usually at once. The unlabeled corpora are genuinely enormous, which is what makes pretraining work (Chapter 3), but the moment you need a *measured* answer — a binding constant, a validated pathogenic call, a functional readout — you fall off a cliff into the hundreds or thousands of examples, most of them the boring negative, each of them noisy, and all of them entangled with the day, the lab, and the cohort that produced them. This chapter is about that cliff. It is the least glamorous material in the book and the most load-bearing, because a model that tops a benchmark on data with these pathologies can be measuring an artifact, and knowing which is the difference between a lead and a liability.

## Sparsity, imbalance, and noise

Start with scale, because the intuition an ML practitioner imports from language or vision is off by orders of magnitude in the wrong direction. A protein language model pretrains on hundreds of millions of sequences, but the *labeled* datasets you fine-tune or evaluate on are tiny by comparison: a deep mutational scan (DMS, Chapter 7) of one protein is a rare luxury at tens of thousands of variants, most assays deliver a few hundred measurements, and a validated set of clinically confirmed pathogenic variants for a gene may number in the dozens. This scarcity is not incidental; every label costs wet-lab time and money, which is precisely why self-supervised transfer and zero-shot scoring matter so much here (Chapters 3 and 7). The reflex to "just get more labeled data" that works in mainstream ML is often not an option — the data does not exist and cannot be cheaply made.

On top of scarcity sits severe **class imbalance**: the case where one label, almost always the negative, vastly outnumbers the rare positive you actually care about — a real binder in a screen, a pathogenic variant among benign ones, a hit among thousands of misses. Ratios of 1-in-100 to 1-in-10,000 are normal. The trap this sets is that plain accuracy becomes meaningless. A variant classifier facing a 1-in-500 pathogenic **base rate** — the background frequency of the positive class — can call everything benign, score 99.8% accurate, and find nothing. What you care about instead lives in the rare class: recall of true positives and precision at your operating point, quantities that a low base rate quietly crushes even when a summary like AUROC looks healthy, because AUROC is insensitive to prevalence while the precision a decision actually depends on is not. This is why AlphaMissense's proteome-wide calibration, not just its ranking, is what makes it clinically usable [@cheng2023], and it is the first thing evaluation (Chapter 16) has to get right.

The third pathology is noise at the source. Assays are noisy measurements of a noisy system, and their **label noise** — error in the labels themselves — is not a rounding detail but a hard ceiling on performance. A useful distinction: a **technical replicate** re-measures the same biological sample and isolates assay and pipeline noise, while a **biological replicate** is an independent sample from a distinct source (a different individual, culture, or animal) and adds real biological variation on top. When technical replicates of a DMS or an expression assay agree only at, say, Spearman 0.6, that noise caps how well any model can score against the labels: a noise-free predictor of the true signal correlates with a single noisy measurement only about as well as the square root of the replicate agreement, roughly 0.77 here, not a perfect 1.0. So a predictor reaching 0.55 against such labels is doing well, not mediocre — disattenuating for the label noise, it tracks the underlying signal at around 0.7 — a fact that reframes the modest-looking numbers on benchmarks like ProteinGym [@notin2023], and one reason property models are worth acting on for ranking even when their absolute scores are unreliable. Even gold-standard clinical labels carry this noise: the same variant is classified differently by different labs often enough that "ground truth" is itself a distribution.

!!! intuition "Intuition"
    Labeled biological data is small, lopsided, and noisy, so the right question is never "what is the accuracy" but "how much better than the base rate, and how close to the assay's own reproducibility ceiling."

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *I have 200 labeled measurements — is that enough to fine-tune a foundation model?* Probably not to fine-tune, but that is the wrong move anyway. The point of pretraining is that you rarely need many labels: take the model's frozen embeddings and train a small head, or use its zero-shot likelihoods directly, and hold out a real test set. With 200 points, a fully fine-tuned model will memorize them, and any impressive number you see is overfitting rather than signal.

## Batch effects and experimental bias

The single most dangerous confounder in biological data is not biological at all. A **batch effect** is systematic technical variation introduced by *when, where, and how* samples were processed — the day, the sequencing lane, the reagent lot, the machine, the technician, the lab — rather than by the biology under study [@leek2010]. Batch effects are pervasive across genomics and single-cell data: profile the same cells on two days or in two labs and they will often separate more cleanly by batch than by cell type. On its own this is a nuisance you can model. It becomes a catastrophe when the batch is **confounded** with the variable you care about — when a variable influences both the input and the label — because then the model can achieve any accuracy you like by learning the batch and ignoring the biology entirely.

The classic way this happens is an experimental design where the confounding is baked in: cases sequenced in one run and controls in another, treated cells harvested on Monday and controls on Friday, one cohort genotyped on an old array and another on a new one. Now "case versus control" and "batch one versus batch two" are the same axis, and no amount of cleverness downstream can separate them. The cautionary tale that travels best comes from medical imaging: a pneumonia-detection network trained across hospitals learned to read the scanner and the department — portable versus fixed machines, metal tokens placed in the image field — rather than the lung pathology, so its accuracy dropped sharply when it met a hospital it had not trained on [@zech2018]. The genomic analogue is a "disease signature" that is really a sample-processing signature, and it fails the same way the moment it leaves the lab that made it.

!!! analogy "Analogy"
    A batch effect is like a photographer who always shoots the healthy patients in daylight and the sick ones under fluorescent light: a model can hit perfect accuracy by learning the color temperature. Where it leaks: lighting is visible and you would notice, whereas a batch effect hides in high-dimensional readouts you cannot eyeball, which is exactly why it slips through.

Ancestry is the same problem wearing a different coat. **Population stratification** — confounding by ancestry, where allele frequencies and disease rates both differ across groups (Chapter 5) — is a batch effect at the level of whole cohorts, and it is why a **polygenic score (PRS)** trained in one ancestry loses much of its accuracy in another: the correlations it learned do not transfer [@martin2019]. Deploy such a score without checking and you can widen the health disparities it was meant to narrow, the concern that runs through variant-to-mechanism (Chapter 13).

The reason batch effects are so treacherous for foundation models specifically is that they attack evaluation from both sides. If train and test share batches, the batch signal leaks across the split and *inflates* your reported performance, so the model looks better than it is. If deployment introduces a new batch — a new lab, a patient's tissue instead of a cell line — you hit a **distribution shift** and performance *collapses*, the failure that haunts cell-engineering models moving off their training cell lines (Chapter 11). The safeguard is to split by batch, site, or individual rather than at random, which is a central discipline of honest evaluation (Chapter 16).

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Believing you can always correct a batch effect after the fact. Tools like ComBat can remove batch variation when batch and the biological signal are separable, but if your design *confounded* them — cases in one batch, controls in another — then any correction that removes the batch also removes the biology, and any signal that survives is suspect. Confounding is prevented at the bench by randomizing and balancing samples across batches, not patched in the analysis.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *A statistician asks: we regressed out the batch variable with ComBat before training, so the batch effect is handled — right?* Only if batch was not confounded with your outcome. Correction assumes the batch and the biology point in different directions so one can be subtracted; when they point the same way by design, subtraction guts the signal too. Show me the design first: were cases and controls balanced across batches? If not, no post-hoc method can rescue it, and the honest answer is to redo the experiment with randomized processing.

## Heterogeneity of effects

The last reality is the subtlest: an average can be a lie. **Effect heterogeneity** is when the same variant, perturbation, or drug produces different effects — sometimes opposite effects — across contexts, so that a single number averaged over them describes no one. Biology is heterogeneity almost by definition. A variant that raises a gene's expression in one cell type can lower it in another; two mutations that each help a protein can combine to break it, the sign **epistasis** of Chapter 7; a perturbation that pushes one genetic background toward a phenotype does nothing in another. The average effect is a real quantity, but it answers a population-level question, and it can be badly misleading the instant you act on an individual.

The sharp version of this is **Simpson's paradox**: a trend that holds inside every subgroup can reverse or vanish once you pool them, because an average over heterogeneous groups need not resemble any group. If a variant's effect on expression is strongly positive in one cell type and strongly negative in another, a model fit to the mixture can report a flat, near-zero effect that is correct on average and wrong everywhere. This is not a corner case in genomics and single-cell biology; it is the default, because cell-type specificity and genetic-background dependence are the norm.

!!! intuition "Intuition"
    The average family has 2.3 children, a number describing no actual family. An averaged biological effect can be exactly this: technically correct, individually useless, and occasionally the reverse of what happens in the subgroup you care about.

This reframes the field's most-cited open gap. Sequence-to-function models predict variation *across* genes and tissues well but explain *inter-individual* variation poorly, often missing even the direction of a personal variant's effect on expression [@huang2023; @sasse2023]. Read through the lens of this section, that gap is a heterogeneity problem: the models learned the strong, shared signal of what a promoter or enhancer does on average, and that signal swamps the small, person-specific deviations that a population-cohort study of an aging disease actually turns on. The average is what they are good at; the individual is where they are weakest, and those are the same fact seen twice. The same logic explains why simple baselines are so hard to beat in perturbation prediction: most perturbations barely move most genes, so predicting the training mean already scores well, and the deep models earn their keep only on the minority of genes that genuinely respond [@ahlmanneltze2025].

The remedy is not a bigger model but a sharper question. Stratify by the contexts you suspect matter — cell type, ancestry, genetic background, sex — model interactions rather than main effects alone, and report per-subgroup rather than pooled numbers. Above all, decide whether the average is even the target: for allocating a screen across a population it may be, but for a single patient or a single cell it usually is not. This is where the noncoding, polygenic architecture of most traits (Chapter 13) and the omnigenic view that thousands of tiny, context-dependent effects sum to a phenotype [@boyle2017] stop being abstractions and start dictating how you evaluate (Chapter 16) and how you close the loop with experiments (Chapter 17).

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *A wet-lab partner asks: your model says this perturbation upregulates my target gene on average — will it in my primary T cells?* Treat "on average" as a warning label, not a reassurance. That average was taken over cell lines and conditions that may not include yours, and effects of this kind flip with cell type more often than newcomers expect. It is a hypothesis worth testing in your context, not a prediction you can port; confirm it in the cells you actually plan to use before you build a program on it.
