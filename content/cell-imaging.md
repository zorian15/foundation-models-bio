A microscope is the oldest instrument in biology, and it is quietly becoming a foundation-model input. Point one at a dish of cells, stain a handful of their parts in different colors, and every cell becomes a picture; measure enough about each picture and the cell becomes a vector of numbers. That vector is a **phenotype** — a readout of how the cell actually looks and behaves — and it is *unbiased* in a specific sense: you did not decide in advance which gene or pathway to watch, you photographed the whole cell and let the differences surface. This appendix is a short tour of that idea. First, what image-based profiling measures and why it is a cheap, broad phenotypic fingerprint of a perturbation; then, the models that turn the pictures into profiles and use them to guess a compound's mechanism. It sits alongside the other reserved-field appendices (single-cell, Appendix A; spatial omics, Appendix B) as a modality the book's main models increasingly fold in.

## What imaging measures

Start with the assay the field has standardized on. **Cell Painting** is a multiplexed fluorescence protocol: you apply six inexpensive dyes that light up eight broadly useful cellular components — the nucleus, nucleoli and cytoplasmic RNA, the endoplasmic reticulum, the Golgi and plasma membrane, actin, and mitochondria — image them across five channels on an automated microscope, and do it for thousands of cells per well in multi-well plates [@bray2016]. Nothing about the dye set is disease-specific; it is a deliberately generic palette meant to expose *whatever* changed. This is **high-content screening**: microscopy scaled to whole plates so that each well is a perturbation and each perturbation yields thousands of imaged cells rather than a single summary number.

The picture is not the product; the profile is. Classical image-analysis software (CellProfiler is the reference tool) segments each cell and measures on the order of 1,500 **morphological features** — sizes, shapes, intensities, textures, and how the stained compartments are arranged relative to one another. Stack those numbers and each cell, or each perturbation averaged over its cells, becomes a high-dimensional **morphological profile**: the phenotypic fingerprint. **Morphological profiling** is the whole pipeline — image, extract features, compare profiles — used to ask which perturbations make cells look alike and which make them look different from untreated controls.

!!! intuition "Intuition"
    A morphological profile is to a cell's *appearance* what a transcriptome is to its *expression*: a high-dimensional readout of state, except you get it from a photograph and a stain rather than from sequencing.

The reason to care is economics and breadth. A single Cell Painting plate profiles a perturbation for a small fraction of the cost of single-cell RNA sequencing (Chapter 4), and it responds to interventions of any kind — a small molecule (Chapter 10) or a genetic edit such as a CRISPR knockout (Chapter 11) — because both ultimately change how the cell looks. The **JUMP-Cell Painting** consortium (Joint Undertaking in Morphological Profiling) industrialized this: its public resource pairs chemical and genetic perturbations across millions of images so that a compound and a gene edit can be compared in the same feature space [@chandrasekaran2024].

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Unbiased" does not mean "interpretable." The profile faithfully records *that* the cell changed, but a 1,500-dimensional feature vector names no gene, target, or pathway on its own. Morphology gives you a phenotype to match and cluster, not a mechanism you can read off a single feature, and treating a striking feature as if it were a target is exactly the leap the models in the next section are built to make — carefully.

## The models

The learned part of the field is the encoder that replaces, or augments, the hand-built CellProfiler features. A **self-supervised** image encoder is trained on the Cell Painting images themselves — no perturbation labels — to produce an embedding per cell or per well, then those embeddings are used as the profile. Trained this way on large screens, encoders learn representations that reproduce known biology at least as well as the engineered features while cutting compute, and they transfer to screens they never saw [@moshkov2024]. Modern self-supervised recipes borrowed from computer vision — DINO, masked autoencoders, SimCLR — push this further, matching or surpassing CellProfiler at grouping perturbations by drug target and gene family without any task labels [@kim2025]; the largest efforts pretrain masked-image foundation models on tens of millions of cell images as general-purpose morphology encoders.

What the profiles are *for* is inference by similarity. Cluster perturbations in embedding space and compounds that share a **mechanism of action** (MoA) — the molecular route by which a drug produces its effect — tend to group together, so an uncharacterized compound inherits a hypothesis from the cluster it lands in. The same move flags likely toxicity (a profile resembling known toxicants) and drives **virtual screening**, ranking a large compound library by how closely each profile matches a desired phenotype. The most powerful version exploits JUMP's matched design: if a compound's morphological profile matches the profile of a specific **gene knockout**, that gene or its pathway becomes a candidate target for the compound [@chandrasekaran2024]. Morphology is, in that sense, one more **modality** to align and fuse with sequence, structure, and expression (Chapter 14), and a fast, cheap readout to close the design-build-test loop with (Chapter 17).

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *A skeptical statistician asks: "Your embeddings cluster beautifully — but are they clustering biology or plates?"* Often the latter, and it is the field's defining hazard. A **batch effect** is systematic technical variation — a different imaging day, plate, microscope, or dye lot — that shifts profiles independently of the biology, and in imaging it is large enough to dominate the perturbation signal and produce clusters that track the experiment's logistics rather than any mechanism. That is why serious pipelines correct for batch explicitly and score on held-out batches, and it is the same messy-data problem the book treats head-on in Chapter 15. A clustering that was never checked against batch is not yet evidence.

Give the approach its due and its limits together. It genuinely surfaces MoA and target hypotheses across thousands of perturbations for the price of imaging, and it does so for the "undruggable" and the uncharacterized alike because it needs no prior model of the target. But every output is a *similarity*, and similarity is correlational: a matched profile is a lead to confirm, not a mechanism established, and the match only exists if the two perturbations produce phenotypes the assay can actually tell apart — many distinct mechanisms look identical under one dye set. Profiles also shift with concentration and timepoint, so "the profile" of a compound is really a family of profiles. The honest framing mirrors the rest of this book: morphological profiling is an excellent, cheap hypothesis generator whose rankings you take to the bench, not a phenotype-to-target oracle.

!!! note "Note"
    Morphology and the transcriptome are complementary readouts of the same perturbation: imaging is cheaper and captures spatial and structural changes a Perturb-seq count matrix (Chapter 11) cannot, while expression names genes that a picture never will. Several groups now profile both and learn maps between them, which is why imaging increasingly shows up as a modality inside the integration models of Chapter 14 rather than as a standalone assay.
