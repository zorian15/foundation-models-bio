This is the last chapter, and it is the most speculative one in the book. Everything before it described models you can download and run today, with benchmark numbers you can check. This chapter is about where the field thinks it is going, which means it trades some of that rigor for a horizon. The organizing dream has a name now: the *virtual cell*, a computable model of a cell you could interrogate the way a physicist queries a simulation, asking "what happens if I perturb this?" and trusting the answer. The rest of this chapter does two things. First it sketches that dream honestly, saying what "virtual cell" actually means and what is being built toward it. Then it does the thing this book has done in every chapter, only pointed at the future: separate what is plausibly imminent from what is science fiction, and name why the hardest gaps are not the kind that scale alone will close.

!!! note "A more speculative chapter"
    Read this one with a lighter grip than the rest. The models named elsewhere in the book ship today; the trajectories named here are forecasts, and forecasts about this field have a poor track record in both directions, over- and under-shooting. Where a claim is aspiration rather than result, the prose says so.

## Where the field is heading

The clearest articulation of the destination is the **AI virtual cell** (AIVC): a multi-scale, multi-modal model that represents the state of a cell and predicts how that state changes under any perturbation, across molecules, cells, and tissues [@bunne2024]. Think of it as the ambition that unifies the whole book. The molecular lobe (Chapters 8 through 10) can predict what a single molecule looks like and binds; the genomic lobe (Chapters 12 and 13) can read what a stretch of DNA does; the perturbation models (Chapter 11) can predict how a cell's transcriptome shifts when you knock a gene out. A virtual cell is what you get if one model holds all of that at once, in one shared representation, so that a genetic change propagates through structure, expression, and cell state the way it does in a living cell. The payoff would be the **in-silico experiment**: running the assay in the model instead of at the bench, screening millions of interventions computationally and taking only the survivors into the lab.

It is worth being precise about the distance to that goal, because the caveat from Chapter 14 is the load-bearing fact here. Today's "multimodal" systems are each unimodal-plus. A co-folder is a structure model that accepts ligands; Evo 2 is a sequence model that spans the central dogma through DNA [@brixi2026]; Arc Institute's State is a perturbation model that maps an intervention to an expression shift [@adduri2025]. None of them is the virtual cell, and the label "virtual cell" attached to any single current model is aspiration borrowing the name of the destination. The field is organizing around the destination, not shipping it.

!!! intuition "Intuition"
    A virtual cell is to today's perturbation models what a flight simulator is to a wind-tunnel test of one wing: the simulator has to get the whole aircraft's behavior right at once, and we currently have very good models of individual parts.

Three concrete trends are the honest content behind the slogan. The first is **scale of context**: models are growing from a gene or a hundred-kilobase window toward whole-genome and whole-cell context, on the bet that regulation that looks unpredictable in a narrow window becomes legible when the model can see everything at once. The second is the **perturbation atlas**: the raw material a virtual cell learns from is paired (perturbation, response) data, and screens like genome-scale Perturb-seq [@replogle2022] and the large compendia behind State [@adduri2025] are the closest thing the field has to the "corpus" that made language models work (Chapter 11). More perturbations, more cell types, more contexts is the data flywheel everyone is betting on. The third is the **self-driving lab**: closing the design-build-test-learn loop (Chapter 17) with robotics so that the model proposes, the robot runs, and the results retrain the model without a human in the middle each cycle.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *If we just keep scaling the perturbation atlases, does the virtual cell fall out the way structure prediction fell out of scaling sequences?* Maybe for the parts that are distributional, not for the parts that are causal. Scaling worked for structure because a protein's fold is a near-deterministic function of its sequence and the training signal (the Protein Data Bank) samples that function densely. A cell's response to a novel perturbation in a cell type you never screened is a causal counterfactual, and no amount of observing the perturbations you *did* run guarantees the ones you did not. Scale buys coverage of the space you sampled; it does not by itself buy extrapolation to genuinely new biology.

## What is science fiction and what is imminent

Hold two lists side by side, because the honest forecast is not one arrow but a fork. Some of what the virtual-cell narrative promises is a straight-line extension of what already works and is plausibly a few years out. Some of it runs into the exact gaps this book has flagged in every chapter, and those gaps are not about compute.

Start with the imminent, because it is real and it is a lot. Co-folding and design keep improving, and better binders, enzymes, and small molecules coming out of models like the co-folders (Chapter 8) and RFdiffusion-class designers (Chapter 9) are a near-certain continuation, not a gamble. Perturbation atlases will keep growing and the models trained on them will keep getting better at ranking what to screen next (Chapter 11). Variant scoring is already deployed as *one line of evidence* in clinical variant interpretation, and its use as a routine input alongside experimental and population evidence will broaden (Chapter 13). And the design-build-test-learn loop will keep accelerating as the harness matures (Chapter 17). None of this requires a breakthrough; it requires the field to keep doing what it is doing.

Now the aspirational, stated plainly as speculation. A *general* virtual cell that predicts arbitrary perturbations across arbitrary cell types is not close: current perturbation models can still trail a predict-the-mean or additive baseline on genuinely unseen perturbations [@ahlmanneltze2025], and cross-cell-type transfer is the open headline problem, not a solved one (Chapter 11). Replacing wet-lab validation is further still, and arguably a category error: the models are trained and judged against wet-lab data, so a model that could replace the bench would first have to be validated by the very bench it replaces. And personal-genome-level prediction, saying what *your* specific variant does to *your* expression, remains open: sequence-to-function models predict variation across genes and tissues well yet explain inter-individual variation poorly, sometimes missing the direction of a personal variant's effect [@huang2023; @sasse2023] (Chapters 12 and 13). These three are not on the same timeline as bigger co-folders, and treating them as if they were is the central way the virtual-cell story gets oversold.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Reading a virtual-cell demo that predicts a held-out perturbation *within* a screened cell line as evidence that arbitrary-perturbation, arbitrary-cell-type prediction is around the corner. The impressive in-distribution result and the aspirational out-of-distribution claim are different problems, and the gap between them is exactly the causal, cross-context extrapolation that stays hard. Ask what was held out before you believe the extrapolation.

The reason these gaps persist is structural, not incidental. They are causal (what does perturbing this actually *do*, versus what correlates with it), in-vivo (a transcriptome shift in a dish is not a phenotype in a body, and neither is a clinical benefit), and personal (the signal that distinguishes two humans is small next to the signal that distinguishes two genes). Each is a place where the training data undersamples the question, and no objective computed on the data you have repairs a question the data does not answer. That is why "just scale it" is a real strategy for the imminent list and a hopeful one for the aspirational list.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *So should I tell my company that in silico will replace our screens?* Tell them it will reorder the screens, not retire them. The value that has actually arrived is triage: the models turn an intractable search space into a ranked shortlist, so the bench spends its budget on candidates that are already enriched for hits. What has not arrived, and may not for a long time, is a model whose prediction you would act on without ever running the assay. Budget for the loop (Chapter 17), not for its disappearance.

This is the through-line to end the book on. Across every chapter, foundation models did the same thing: they moved the bottleneck. The hard part of biology used to be *proposing* candidates, a target worth drugging, a protein that might fold, a variant that might matter, and generating good hypotheses was slow and expensive. Foundation models made proposing cheap and fast, and in doing so they relocated the bottleneck to *testing and validating* those proposals well, honestly, in the right system. That is not a smaller job; it is a different one, and it is the one this book has been quietly arguing is now the important one. The reader who takes the most from all this is not the one who can name the newest model. It is the one who can stand at the seam between the biology and the machine learning, read a benchmark for what it does and does not answer, design the evaluation that actually settles the question, and know that when the model has spoken, the bench still writes the verdict.
