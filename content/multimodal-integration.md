A single mutation can break a protein's shape, or it can leave the protein untouched and instead change how much of it a cell makes. From the outside, both look like "this gene now causes disease," and you often cannot tell which mechanism is at work without looking at the molecule and the regulation at the same time. That is the problem this chapter is about. The book has run on two tracks so far: a molecular lobe (Chapters 6 through 11) that folds and designs single molecules, and a genomic lobe (Chapters 12 and 13) that reads DNA and predicts what it does. The central dogma ties them together, and the models in this chapter are the first ones that try to reason across the tie. This is the book's bridge, and it is also its most honest chapter: the unified-model story is compelling, genuinely working in narrow places, and oversold everywhere else.

## Why one modality is never enough

Start with a variant near a gene and one question: what does it do? The molecular lobe has a view. If the variant is a **missense** change (it swaps one amino acid for another), a protein language model or a structure predictor can tell you whether the swap destabilizes the fold or reshapes an active site (Chapters 7 and 8). The genomic lobe has a different view. If the variant sits in a promoter or enhancer, a sequence-to-function model can tell you whether it turns the gene's expression up or down (Chapter 12). Each view is blind to the other's question. A structure model has nothing to say about dosage; a regulatory model has nothing to say about whether the protein it counts still works. Worse, the two mechanisms masquerade as each other: a variant that destabilizes a protein gets it degraded, which shows up as *less protein*, which looks exactly like a regulatory effect measured by expression alone. Localize the mechanism to the wrong lobe and you nominate the wrong drug strategy.

This is why the field wants **multimodal integration**: models that reason jointly over more than one kind of biological measurement, rather than one modality in isolation. A **modality** is a distinct type of data, such as amino-acid sequence, 3D structure, DNA sequence, or single-cell expression (Chapter 4). No single modality is the whole cell. Sequence is cheap and abundant but silent about the shapes and quantities it gives rise to; structure is a frozen snapshot of one molecule; expression counts molecules but forgets what they look like. The synthesis premise from Chapter 1 finally cashes out here: proteins and genomes are two faces of one system, and the interesting biology lives at the seam.

!!! intuition "Intuition"
    A modality is one instrument's reading; a cell is the patient, and no single instrument localizes the problem, so you have to fuse the readings.

!!! analogy "Analogy"
    Multimodal integration is like a clinical workup: the blood panel, the scan, and the history each rule some things in and out, and only together do they point at a diagnosis. Where it leaks: in the clinic every test is run on the *same patient* on the same day, whereas in biology the sequence, the structure, and the expression are usually measured on different samples in different labs, so "the same cell, seen every way" is a luxury you rarely actually have. That gap is the whole difficulty of the next two sections.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *If more views are better, why not just assay everything on every sample and skip the modeling?* Because paired measurements are rare and expensive. Assays consume the sample, run on different platforms, and cost real money and time each, so the number of samples measured in *all* modalities at once is tiny next to the number measured in any *one*. Models earn their keep precisely by transferring from the abundant single-modality data to the scarce paired data, not by waiting for an all-assays dataset that mostly does not exist.

## How models fuse modalities

Before the biology, the vocabulary. There are only a few ways to combine modalities, and the machine-learning machinery behind them is Chapter 3's job; here is the intuition-level map. **Early fusion** joins the raw inputs at the very start and lets one network learn over the combined thing. **Late fusion** runs a separate model per modality and combines their outputs at the end. In between sit the two ideas that most modern models actually use: a **shared latent space**, where modality-specific encoders each map their input into one common embedding space so that a protein and the gene that encodes it land near each other, and **cross-attention**, where one modality's representation is allowed to attend to another's (the attention operation of Chapter 3, but reaching across modalities instead of within one sequence). A common way to *build* a shared latent space is **contrastive alignment**: train on known pairs so that matching items from two modalities are pulled together and mismatched ones pushed apart, the trick behind text-image models, reused here for biological pairs. Real systems mix these; the labels are a way to reason about a design, not rigid boxes.

Biology has produced three integration strategies worth knowing by name, each a different answer to "what counts as one input."

The first is **joint co-folding**: fold a protein together with its partners in a single network. AlphaFold3 [@abramson2024] and the open-weight co-folders Boltz [@wohlwend2024; @passaro2025] and Chai-1 [@chai2024] take a protein plus any nucleic acids, small-molecule **ligands**, and ions and predict one joint structure for the whole assembly, working at the level of individual atoms so that a drug and its pocket are folded *at the same time* rather than docked afterward (Chapters 8 and 10). RoseTTAFold All-Atom [@krishna2024] reaches the same modality breadth by a different, non-diffusion route. In the diffusion co-folders this is early fusion made physical: every component is thrown into one denoising process, and the model has to satisfy all of them jointly.

The second is a **unified sequence stream**: make everything one alphabet. Evo 2 [@brixi2026] is trained on 9.3 trillion nucleotides of DNA across all domains of life, and because the central dogma runs *through* DNA, a single DNA model implicitly spans RNA and protein too: its context contains the coding regions that become proteins and the regulatory regions that set expression. The elegance is that there is no fusion step at all, because there is only ever one token type; the model's own interpretability shows it discovering exon-intron boundaries, transcription-factor sites, and protein structural elements on its own (Chapter 12). The catch, worth stating plainly, is that "spans DNA, RNA, and protein" means it learns features of all three *from sequence* — it does not ingest a 3D structure or an expression matrix.

The third is a **cross-modal map**: learn to translate one modality into another. Perturbation-response models take an intervention in one modality (a genetic or chemical perturbation) and predict a readout in a different one (the transcriptome shift), trained on Perturb-seq pairs [@replogle2022] where each cell links a perturbation to its expression response (Chapter 11). GEARS [@roohani2023] represents a perturbation by its neighborhood in a gene-gene graph; scGPT-perturb fine-tunes the scGPT single-cell foundation model [@cui2024]; Arc Institute's State model [@adduri2025] embeds transcriptomes into a noise-robust latent space with one module and predicts the perturbation-driven shift over that space with another. All three are the same shape: perturbation in, expression out.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *These modalities have wildly different noise and scale. How does a shared latent space cope?* It mostly does not, for free. Expression is count noise and heavy batch effects, structure is near-deterministic coordinates, sequence is discrete and clean, so one modality can swamp the others in a naively shared space. That is exactly what a State-style embedding module is *for* — it maps raw transcriptomes into a space engineered to be more invariant to technical noise before any cross-modal prediction happens. Alignment is a modeling problem you pay for, not a property you get by concatenating.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Reading "multimodal" as "uses several data types anywhere in the pipeline." Bolting a separate structure predictor and a separate expression predictor together and averaging their scores is late fusion, and it never lets one modality *inform* another. The interesting claim is a model where the modalities share representation and can constrain each other; check which one a paper actually built before you believe the synthesis story.

## What they do well and what is still hard

Give the wins their due, because they are real. Joint co-folding genuinely changed structure-based drug work: on standard benchmarks the co-folders predict a ligand's bound pose more accurately than out-of-the-box blind docking tools like AutoDock Vina, though a docking baseline handed a known pocket stays competitive, and they do it while folding the pocket and the ligand together, which sidesteps the old assumption of a rigid receptor [@abramson2024]. And a single model that spans the central dogma is not a slogan: Evo 2 scores coding *and* noncoding variants from one set of weights, trained across all domains of life [@brixi2026], where the field used to need a different specialized model for each. One network, many modalities, is a capability that did not exist a few years ago.

Now the honest boundary, and it is wide. The first limit is that a benchmark win on co-folding is partly a memorization win. Careful audits find that these models often reproduce poses they effectively saw in training and generalize weakly to genuinely novel targets, and a large fraction of predicted poses fail basic physical-validity checks — wrong stereochemistry, clashes, impossible geometry — even when the coordinates look plausible [@buttenschoen2024]. "Beats docking on the benchmark" and "trustworthy on your new target" are different claims.

The deeper limits are about data, not networks. **Paired data** — samples measured in more than one modality at once — is the scarce resource, and the fully paired corner of the matrix above is tiny. That forces every integration model to lean on **missing-modality inference**: predicting or imputing the modalities a sample lacks, which works when modalities are redundant and fails silently when the missing one carried the signal you needed. Modalities also live at different resolutions and noise scales, so aligning them is a modeling cost you pay rather than a free lunch, and the alignment can be wrong in ways no single-modality metric will catch. Evaluation itself gets harder across modalities, because a model can look right on the modality you can measure while being wrong on the one you cannot — the theme of Chapter 16. And the field's favorite failure recurs here: perturbation-response models can trail simple mean-and-additive baselines on truly unseen perturbations [@ahlmanneltze2025], just as sequence-to-function models nail cross-gene variation while missing variation across individuals [@huang2023; @sasse2023]. Fusing modalities does not repeal those gaps; it inherits all of them at once.

!!! collaborator "Collaborator"
    *So is there a single model that reasons over DNA, structure, and cell state together yet?* No, and it is worth being precise about why not. Today's "multimodal" systems are each unimodal-plus: a co-folder is a structure model that accepts ligands, Evo 2 is a sequence model that spans the dogma through DNA, a perturbation model maps one modality to another. None carries genome, protein structure, and cell state in one shared representation. The one-model-to-rule-them-all cell is an aspiration the field is organizing around, not a product you can run — the frontier Chapter 18 takes up.

!!! intuition "Intuition"
    Integration multiplies capability and multiplies failure modes at the same time; the win is a model that sees more, and the risk is a model that is confidently wrong in a way no single-modality check can flag.

The mental model to carry out of Part IV: the two lobes of this book really are one system, and the first models that span them — co-folders, unified sequence streams, perturbation maps — are genuine and genuinely narrow. Each fuses two or three modalities for one task, none fuses all of them, and the thing standing between here and the unified cell is not mainly a cleverer architecture but paired data, honest cross-modal evaluation, and a healthy distrust of the synthesis narrative the field most wants to believe.
